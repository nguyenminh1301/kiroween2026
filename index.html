Apollo 11 · July 16 to 24, 1969

Eight days to the Moon and back

Apollo 11 left Florida on July 16, 1969, and came down in the Pacific Ocean on July 24. Two of its crew walked on the Moon. This is the flight, from the launch pad to the recovery ship.

At 9:32 on the morning of July 16, 1969, a Saturn V rocket left Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center in Florida. Three men were aboard. Eight days, three hours and eighteen minutes later they were back, in the Pacific Ocean. In between, two of them had stood on the Moon.

The mission was Apollo 11. People remember it for the landing, but the flight had many parts, and each one had to work. The sections below follow them in order.

The crew

Neil A. Armstrong was the commander. Edwin E. “Buzz” Aldrin Jr. was the lunar module pilot. Michael Collins was the command module pilot.

The spacecraft had two parts that matter here. The command module was named Columbia. The lunar module was named Eagle. Armstrong and Aldrin would fly Eagle down to the Moon. Collins would stay in Columbia.

NASA named the three as the prime crew of the landing mission, and this portrait is dated May 1969.

Launch

Apollo 11 lifted off at 9:32 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time on July 16, 1969. The launch site was Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center. The rocket was a Saturn V.

The Saturn V and the spacecraft on top of it stood 363 feet tall, about 110 metres.

About three minutes into the flight, just after the second stage lit, the launch escape tower and the boost protective cover were jettisoned. The cover had shielded the command module and its windows until then.

Three days out

After launch, Apollo 11 headed for the Moon. The trip took three days.

The spacecraft went into orbit around the Moon on July 19. One more day in orbit followed, and then the landing on July 20.

In lunar orbit Columbia passed behind the Moon again and again. Each pass cut the radio link to Earth. The section on Collins explains what that meant.

The landing

Armstrong and Aldrin landed Eagle in the Sea of Tranquility on July 20, 1969, at 4:17 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time.

The descent did not go smoothly. Eagle’s computer gave 1202 and 1201 program alarms on the way down. It was short of time for its jobs and dropped the low priority ones. Houston called it go, and the landing went on.

Near the surface Armstrong flew past a boulder field by hand. Then Eagle was down.

“Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.”

Armstrong, July 20, 1969

On the surface

Armstrong stepped onto the Moon at 10:56 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time on July 20. Aldrin also walked on the surface.

“That’s one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.”

Armstrong, July 20, 1969

The moonwalk lasted about two and a half hours.

The crew set up a flag, a passive seismometer, a laser reflector and a solar wind sheet. They collected about 21.5 kilograms of samples.

The mission left a plaque on the Moon. It reads: “Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the Moon, July 1969, A.D. We came in peace for all mankind.”

Eagle lifted off on July 21 after about 21.5 hours on the surface. The ascent engine’s exhaust knocked the flag over.

Alone in orbit

While Armstrong and Aldrin were on the surface, Collins stayed in lunar orbit aboard Columbia.

Each time Columbia passed behind the Moon, Collins lost radio contact with Earth. The loss lasted about 48 minutes on every orbit. For that time nobody on Earth could hear him.

After liftoff, the ascent stage of Eagle flew back to Columbia and docked with it.

Coming home

Columbia came down in the Pacific Ocean on July 24, 1969. The capsule landed about 24 kilometres from the recovery ship USS Hornet.

The crew went into quarantine after the recovery. The mission had lasted 8 days, 3 hours and 18 minutes.

What really happened

This site also has a game, Loss of Signal. The game is fiction, but it shows real moments of this flight. Below are eight of them: what you see in the game, what really happened, and where it is written down.

The marks show what you saw in your last flight on this browser.

  1. The escape tower leaves

    In the game After the second stage lit, the launch escape tower and its cover left the capsule. The windows were clear.

    What really happened The launch escape tower and the boost protective cover were jettisoned about three minutes into the flight, just after the second stage lit. The cover had shielded the command module and its windows until then.

    Source: Apollo 11 Flight Journal

  2. Loss of signal

    In the game The ship passed behind the Moon and Houston could not be heard.

    What really happened Behind the Moon the spacecraft had no radio contact with Earth at all, about 48 minutes of every orbit.

    Source: Apollo 11 Flight Journal

  3. The 1202 alarm

    In the game A program alarm, a 1202, lit the guidance computer display during the powered descent.

    What really happened Eagle’s computer gave 1202 and 1201 alarms on the way down. It was short of time for its jobs and dropped the low priority ones. Houston called it go and the landing went on.

    Source: Apollo 11 Flight Journal

  4. The Eagle has landed

    In the game The commander called Houston after the contact light: the Eagle has landed.

    What really happened Armstrong said “Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.” at about 20:17 UTC on July 20, 1969, in the Sea of Tranquility.

    Source: Apollo Lunar Surface Journal

  5. The seismometer hears footsteps

    In the game Houston said the seismometer was already picking up the crew’s footsteps.

    What really happened The seismometer left on the Moon, the Passive Seismic Experiment Package, recorded the astronauts’ footsteps. It ran for about three weeks.

    Source: Apollo Lunar Surface Journal

  6. The flag falls

    In the game The ascent engine fired and the flag fell over.

    What really happened Aldrin reported that the blast of the ascent engine knocked the flag over. It had a horizontal rod along the top edge to hold the cloth out, because there is no wind on the Moon.

    Source: Apollo 11 Flight Journal

  7. The ascent engine ignites

    In the game The ascent engine fired straight down into the descent stage, with it on top, and a green readout named a routine.

    What really happened The lunar module used its descent stage as a launch pad: the ascent engine fired down onto it. The guidance computer routine that ignited the engines is named BURN_BABY_BURN--MASTER_IGNITION_ROUTINE.

    Source: Apollo Guidance Computer source, Luminary 099

  8. USS Hornet and Helicopter 66

    In the game USS Hornet and a Sea King helicopter, Helicopter 66, came for the capsule after splashdown.

    What really happened Columbia landed in the Pacific on July 24, 1969, about 24 km (13 nautical miles) from the recovery carrier USS Hornet. Swimmers opened the hatch and Helicopter 66 flew the crew to the ship.

    Source: Apollo 11 Flight Journal
