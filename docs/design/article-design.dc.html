The Moon landing, 1969

Eight Days to the Moon and Back

Apollo 11 left Florida on July 16, 1969, and came down in the Pacific Ocean on July 24. Two of its crew walked on the Moon. This is the whole flight, from the launch pad to the recovery ship.

Written for the Loss of Signal project. Every fact is sourced at the end.

At 9:32 on the morning of July 16, 1969, a Saturn V rocket left Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center in Florida with three men aboard. Eight days, three hours and eighteen minutes later they were back, floating in the Pacific Ocean. In between, two of them had stood on the Moon.

People remember the mission for one moment on the surface. But the flight had many parts, and every one of them had to work. The sections below follow them in order.

Part I

The crew

Neil A. Armstrong was the commander. Edwin E. "Buzz" Aldrin Jr. was the lunar module pilot. Michael Collins was the command module pilot.

The spacecraft came in two parts. The command module was named Columbia, and the lunar module was named Eagle. Armstrong and Aldrin would fly Eagle down to the surface. Collins would stay in Columbia, alone in orbit, and wait for them.

Part II

Launch, and three days out

Apollo 11 lifted off at 9:32 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time. The Saturn V and the spacecraft on top of it stood 363 feet tall, about 110 metres.

About three minutes into the flight, just after the second stage lit, the launch escape tower and the boost protective cover were thrown clear. The cover had shielded the command module and its windows until then. For the first time, the crew could see out.

The trip to the Moon took three days. On July 19 the spacecraft went into orbit around it. One more day of checks followed, and then the landing.

Part III

The landing

Armstrong and Aldrin brought Eagle down in the Sea of Tranquility on July 20, 1969, at 4:17 p.m.

The descent did not go smoothly. On the way down, Eagle's computer raised 1202 and 1201 program alarms. It had more work than time and was dropping its low priority jobs. In Houston, the controllers judged that it could carry on, and called it go.

Near the surface the computer was steering toward a field of boulders. Armstrong took over by hand and flew past it. Then Eagle was down.

"Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed."

Neil Armstrong, July 20, 1969

Part IV

On the surface

Armstrong stepped onto the Moon at 10:56 p.m. on July 20. "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." Aldrin followed him down the ladder.

The moonwalk lasted about two and a half hours. They set up a flag, a passive seismometer, a laser reflector and a sheet to catch the solar wind, and they collected about 21.5 kilograms of rock and soil.

The crew left a plaque on one of Eagle's legs. It reads: "Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the Moon, July 1969, A.D. We came in peace for all mankind."

Eagle lifted off on July 21, after about 21.5 hours on the surface. The exhaust of its ascent engine knocked the flag over.

Part V

Alone in orbit

While Armstrong and Aldrin walked on the surface, Michael Collins circled the Moon alone in Columbia.

Each time Columbia passed behind the Moon, its radio link to Earth was cut. The silence lasted about 48 minutes on every orbit. For that time, nobody on Earth could hear him, and he could hear nobody.

On July 21, Eagle's ascent stage flew back up to him and docked.

Part VI

Coming home

Columbia came down in the Pacific Ocean on July 24, 1969, about 24 kilometres from the recovery ship USS Hornet. Swimmers reached the capsule, and a helicopter carried the crew to the ship.

The three men went straight into quarantine, in case they had brought anything back from the Moon. The mission had lasted 8 days, 3 hours and 18 minutes.
